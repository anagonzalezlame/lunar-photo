01

Methodology

How the index is built, and why these three dimensions carry the weight they do.

The project frames Earth as an early, low-cost engineering test bed for permanent lunar base hardware — a shift-left approach, surfacing failures cheaply and early rather than inside a cryogenic vacuum chamber. Rather than seeking a geological clone of Shackleton crater, it identifies Svalbard as an operational proxy: a place real equipment can be tested under some of the same constraints a lunar south-pole mission will face.

The Similarity Index is a weighted average of three per-dimension scores (each 0–1), normalized linearly and treated as independent — no cross-interactions between terrain, ice, and light are modeled:

Score = (ice_score × 0.4) + (illumination_score × 0.3) + (terrain_score × 0.3)

Why these weights

Weights reflect how critical each factor is for a permanent base, not how well each one happens to score. Terrain and ice were both raised from an earlier draft (ice 0.5 → 0.4, terrain 0.2 → 0.3) after reasoning that mobility and energy are prerequisites: a rover that cannot descend a slope, or a habitat that loses power in extended darkness, never gets the chance to use any ice it finds. Illumination stayed at 0.3 throughout.

Methodology note (as submitted): "The proposed Similarity Index assumes a linear relationship in variable normalization and treats each dimension (terrain, illumination, resources) as independent, without modeling cross-interactions. This allows fast quantification to prioritize test sites, but has strict limitations. The model does not capture the effect of reduced gravity (0.16g), the total absence of atmosphere, lunar dust abrasiveness, or Shackleton crater's cryogenic vacuum conditions. Results should therefore be read as an indicator of logistics suitability for early-stage engineering trials, not as absolute geomorphological equivalence."

Beyond this comparison

Beyond this single comparison, the weighted framework is reusable — any future candidate Earth analog, for the Moon or Mars, can be scored the same way. This turns ad hoc site selection into a repeatable, data-driven process.

Next steps toward operational use: model cross-dimension interactions instead of treating them independently, and validate scores against in-situ engineering data as it becomes available.

Where the ice score is softest: ShadowCam's preliminary pass found no exposed surface ice on Shackleton's floor. Mini-RF's detection limit sits around 20–30% by weight and cannot rule out ice at lower concentrations — Zuber et al. (2012) note the true amount could be zero. LCROSS's strongest confirmed water detection was at Cabeus crater, not Shackleton. The 1.00 score measures that the lunar range sits fully inside Svalbard's range — it is a test of whether ice-handling procedures can be rehearsed at all, not a claim that Shackleton's deposit matches Svalbard's in form. Svalbard's ice is solid and massive; Shackleton's, if present, is almost certainly trace-level and mixed into regolith, which would call for thermal sublimation rather than drilling.

04

Historical context

The crater and the archipelago share more history than the comparison alone suggests.

1918

Ernest Shackleton was offered command of a Northern Exploration Company expedition to Spitsbergen (Svalbard). He fell suddenly ill crossing the Arctic Circle for the first time, near Tromsø, and had to turn back; Frank Wild took over. Shackleton himself never reached Svalbard — the crater that bears his name did.

1912

Shackleton's ship Endurance was originally built as the Polaris for Belgian explorer Adrien de Gerlache, intended for a voyage to Spitsbergen. On the Moon, the ridge connecting Shackleton crater to the nearby de Gerlache crater is one of the Artemis program's priority landing sites.

1926

Roald Amundsen and Umberto Nobile departed Ny-Ålesund aboard the airship Norge on their flight to the North Pole. Both men have lunar craters named after them near the south pole; "Nobile Rim 1" is among the polar sites mapped by NASA Goddard.

1922 / 1928

Shackleton died aboard the Quest in 1922. That same ship took part in the 1928 rescue of survivors from the airship Italia, which had departed from Ny-Ålesund.

05

Precedent

Using polar Earth environments as space-hardware test beds is not a new idea.

AMASE — Arctic Mars Analog Svalbard Expedition (2003–2011)

Funded by NASA and ESA. Validated the SAM and CheMin instruments in Svalbard before they flew on the Curiosity rover to Mars.

Haughton-Mars Project — NASA / SETI Institute

Devon Island, Canadian Arctic. Tests spacesuit and rover logistics under polar isolation, the same category of problem this project applies to Svalbard.
